The rooms

The three rooms are one circle in three materials. They share one drone, one root note and one scale, so anything played in one room is in tune with the others, and sound started in one room carries on when you switch.

Water

A pool seen from above. Tap to strike a bowl or bell; hold and circle to make a bowl sing. Low notes are at the centre and high notes at the edge. Stones ring when a ripple reaches them, so where you place the stones decides the pattern that follows each strike. Draws on singing bowls and East Asian temple bells.

Sand

A metal plate covered in sand. When a plate vibrates, some lines on it stay still; sand is shaken off the moving parts and collects on those lines. Every overtone of the drone has its own pattern of still lines. The ring picks which overtone is brought forward, so the figure in the sand is a picture of what you are hearing. Dragging through the sand plays the plate directly. Draws on the tanpura drone and overtone singing.

Glass

A rose window. Each pane is a bell and lights for as long as the bell rings. Every note you play is answered by a second, quieter bell chosen by a fixed rule (see tintinnabuli below). The centre holds the drone. Changes rings the middle ring by itself in the order used by English bell ringers. Draws on church bells, the drone under chant, and cathedral reverberation.

Figures marked "estimate" below are starting points tuned by ear, not measurements. A list of items taken from general knowledge and not checked against a source is at the end.

Buddhism

What the sounds mean

  • Impermanence. A bell's sound arises, changes and fades. Its decay is used as a direct illustration of impermanence (anicca).
  • The mindfulness bell. In Thich Nhat Hanh's Plum Village practice the bell is "invited", not "struck". On hearing it everyone stops and takes about three breaths.
  • Marking time. Bells mark the start and end of sitting and divide the monastic day.

Singing bowls

  • The "Tibetan singing bowl" as a meditation or healing object is largely a Western framing from the 1970s. There is little evidence that bowls were played this way in Tibetan ritual, and monks raised in Tibet report never having seen it. The bowls are real Himalayan bronze vessels, mostly from Nepal and North India, probably used for food, offerings or alms. This app labels them "Singing bowl".
  • The rim vibrates like a ring. Its overtones are not whole-number multiples of the lowest note: thin-ring theory gives 1 : 2.83 : 5.42 : 8.77 : 12.87, and measured bowls sit within a few per cent of that.
  • No bowl is perfectly round, so each overtone splits into two tones a fraction of a hertz to a few hertz apart. The pair drifts in and out of step, which is the slow "wah-wah" pulse. Higher overtones pulse faster.
  • Striking excites many overtones at once: bright at first, then the upper ones fade within seconds while the lowest lasts 15 to 60 seconds. Rubbing the rim drives mainly the lowest tone by stick-slip friction, builds over 2 to 5 seconds, and pulses at about the speed the stick circles. Pressing too hard makes it rattle.

Japanese and Korean bells

  • Rin (keisu). A bowl bell on a cushion, struck in Zen and Pure Land services to mark the start and end of chanting and sitting. Acoustically the same as a singing bowl, but struck, not rubbed.
  • Bonshō. The large temple bell, struck from outside with a hanging wooden beam; there is no clapper. Its sound has three phases: the impact (about 2 to 3 seconds), a bright resonance (up to about 10 seconds) and a long low tail (up to about a minute). The Japanese names for the last two are used inconsistently between sources.
  • Joya no kane. On New Year's Eve temple bells are rung 108 times, once for each of the 108 worldly desires. The practice came from Song-dynasty Chinese Zen in the Kamakura period.
  • Korean bells. The Bell of King Seongdeok has two main tones at 64.18 and 64.52 Hz. They produce one slow throb every 2.94 seconds, which Koreans call maeknori, "pulse". The temple bell here uses that rate.

Other instruments

  • Tingsha. Two small thick cymbals on a cord, struck edge to edge to open or close meditation. High and piercing (about 1.5 to 4 kHz). The two are slightly out of tune with each other, so the pair shimmers quickly (estimate: 3 to 15 pulses a second).
  • Bell and vajra (dril-bu and dorje). Held together in Tibetan ritual. The bell stands for wisdom and emptiness, the vajra for compassion and method.
  • Gyuto overtone chant. Monks chant a very low note (about 60 to 75 Hz) and shape the mouth so one overtone, usually the fifth or tenth, stands out as a second audible note. The ring in the Sand room does the same thing to the drone.

Hinduism

What the sounds mean

  • Nāda Brahma. The doctrine that the world is sound.
  • Struck and unstruck sound. Āhata nāda is ordinary audible sound, made by striking. Anāhata nāda is "unstruck" sound, heard inwardly in yoga. Anāhata is also the name of the heart chakra.
  • Om (AUM). In the Māṇḍūkya Upanishad, A is waking, U is dreaming and M is deep sleep. The fourth part is the silence after M (turīya), pure awareness. The silence is part of the syllable.
  • Bija mantras. Single-syllable seed sounds (LAM, VAM, RAM, YAM, HAM, OM). They are syllables, not frequencies.

The tanpura

  • Four strings, usually tuned Pa, Sa, Sa, low Sa (fifth, octave, octave, root). For ragas without a fifth, Pa is replaced with Ma (the fourth). Each string is plucked in turn, open, in a cycle of about 4 to 6 seconds. The Second control in the Sand room switches between Pa and Ma.
  • The bridge is broad and slightly curved, with a cotton thread under the string, so the string grazes the bridge as it vibrates (jivari). This brings out a nearly complete series of overtones, and a band of emphasis sweeps downward through them over a second or two after each pluck. That falling shimmer is the tanpura's character. The Tanpura drone here reproduces it.
  • Every note of a raga is heard against this unchanging Sa and Pa.

Raga and time of day

Ragas are traditionally assigned to parts of the day (eight watches of about three hours). Assignments vary by school. The scale this app starts on follows the local hour: Bhairav at dawn, Bhoopali by day, Yaman in the evening, Malkauns at night.

Temple sounds

  • Ghanta. The hand bell rung during puja to wake the deity and focus the mind.
  • Shankha. The conch, blown at the start of rituals; a single breathy horn note likened to Om.

Christianity

Church bells

  • A tuned church bell has five main tones: the hum (an octave below), the prime, the tierce, the quint (a fifth) and the nominal (an octave above). The tierce is a minor third, which is why a bell sounds faintly minor whatever is played on it. The hum lasts longest.
  • The note you hear at the strike is not one of the bell's physical tones. The ear infers it from the upper ones, about an octave below the nominal.
  • Change ringing. English bells swing full circle and are rung in changing orders, never repeating a row. On seven bells there are 5,040 possible rows; ringing them all takes about three hours. Plain hunt, used by Changes in the Glass room, is the simplest pattern: each bell moves one place at a time through the order and back.
  • The Angelus. Three strokes, pause, three strokes, pause, three strokes, then nine. Rung at 6 am, noon and 6 pm.
  • Russian zvon. The bells hang still and the clappers are pulled by ropes. The result is layered rhythm, not melody. The four tolls are blagovest (single strokes on the largest bell), trezvon (all bells, festive), perezvon (each bell in turn, largest to smallest) and perebor (single descending strokes, then all together, for funerals).

Chant and drone

  • Gregorian chant. One unaccompanied line in free rhythm, in phrases about a breath long (5 to 10 seconds). It uses eight modes, each with a final note and a reciting note.
  • Ison. In Byzantine chant a second group of singers holds a drone on the home note of the mode, moving it when the melody changes its centre.
  • Organum. The earliest written harmony (about 900): a second voice in parallel fourths or fifths below the chant.

The building

Sound in a large Gothic cathedral takes 5 to 10 seconds to die away; St Paul's in London takes about 11 seconds when empty. Low sounds ring longer than high ones. A parish church takes 2 to 4 seconds. Slow music suits these buildings because fast music blurs. The Space control uses a generated 10-second room with a darker tail.

Tintinnabuli

Arvo Pärt's method, named after the Latin for "little bells". Two voices move together. The melody voice moves by step through a scale. The second voice may only sound the three notes of one chord (the home triad), and at each moment takes the nearest chord note above the melody, or the nearest below, never the same note. In the Glass room, T-voice Above and Below choose which. The answering pane lights in a paler tone so the rule can be seen.

Music of the spheres

Pythagoras tied consonance to simple ratios: 2:1 the octave, 3:2 the fifth, 4:3 the fourth. Boethius extended this to a harmony of the cosmos, and Kepler assigned each planet a range of notes in 1619. It is a philosophical idea, not an acoustic one.

Common threads

  • The drone as ground. Tanpura, ison, the organ pedal note and the hum of Om are all a fixed reference that melody is heard against. Here the drone is shared by every room.
  • The bell as a call to attention. The Angelus, the mindfulness bell, the blagovest and the rin before sitting all ask the listener to stop.
  • Decay into silence. The long tail of a temple bell, the silence after Om and the teaching of impermanence all treat the fading and the silence as part of the sound. Nothing here cuts off; every sound is left to ring out.
  • Breath length. Chant phrases, the three breaths after a bell and the tanpura cycle all last 4 to 10 seconds. Breath in the Sand room cycles at about five and a half per minute.
  • Simple ratios as natural order. Pythagorean tuning, the Indian system of 22 shrutis and the ideal church bell all aim at whole-number ratios.

How the sounds are built

Nothing is sampled. Each bell is a set of sine tones, one per overtone, each with its own loudness and decay time. Where a real instrument pulses, the overtone is two tones a small distance apart. Decay times are for a note at 220 Hz; lower notes ring longer.

Tingsha

Overtones at 1, 2.4, 4.1 and 5.9, with the lowest split by 5.5 Hz so the pair shimmers. Estimates.

Drone

Four sustained strings (root twice, slightly apart; the second string; the octave) each carrying up to 32 overtones. A narrow resonance sits on one overtone and brings it forward; the ring in the Sand room moves it. The Tanpura drone adds a pluck every 1.2 seconds or so, each with a band of emphasis that falls from about the fourteenth overtone to the third over 2.6 seconds.

Tuning

All scales use just intonation: each note is a whole-number ratio above the root, so every note lines up with overtones of the drone and chords do not waver. The five-note scales have no semitone or tritone, so any combination of their notes is consonant.

Claims this app does not make

  • 432 Hz. No robust evidence that it is better than 440 Hz. The usual citation is one small pilot study (33 people) showing a slight difference in heart rate. The story that 440 Hz was a Nazi choice is a myth.
  • "Solfeggio frequencies" (396, 417, 528, 639, 741, 852 Hz). These come from numerology published between the 1970s and 1990s. Medieval note names had no fixed frequencies. The claim that 528 Hz repairs DNA is unsupported.
  • Chakra frequency tables. The tantric sources give syllables, colours and elements for chakras, not frequencies. The tables in circulation contradict each other.
  • Binaural beats. The effect is real but needs headphones and low tones. Evidence that it changes brain rhythms is inconsistent: a 2023 review found 5 of 14 studies in support and 8 against.
  • Singing bowl healing. Only small, mostly uncontrolled studies of mood exist.

The app describes itself as relaxing and makes no health claims.
